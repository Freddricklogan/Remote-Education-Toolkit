# Remote Teaching Best Practices Guide

## Introduction

Remote and hybrid teaching presents unique challenges that differ fundamentally from in-person instruction. The physical separation between educator and students requires deliberate strategies to maintain engagement, build community, ensure equitable access, and achieve learning outcomes. This guide provides evidence-based best practices for educators navigating the remote teaching landscape.

These practices are not theoretical -- they are drawn from the collective experience of thousands of educators who have taught remotely, refined through research and iterative improvement. Whether you are new to remote teaching or looking to improve your existing practice, this guide provides actionable strategies you can implement immediately.

---

## Part 1: Setting Up for Success

### 1.1 Technology Preparation

**Before the First Class:**
- Test your camera, microphone, and speakers with a colleague (not alone -- you need someone to confirm audio and video quality from the receiving end)
- Identify a dedicated teaching space with minimal background distractions, adequate lighting (natural light facing you, not behind you), and a stable internet connection
- Use a wired ethernet connection whenever possible; Wi-Fi introduces latency and reliability issues that compound during live instruction
- Prepare a backup plan: mobile hotspot, phone dial-in number, pre-recorded content that can be deployed if live instruction fails
- Test screen sharing with your actual teaching materials, not just a sample document
- Verify that your learning management system (LMS), video conferencing tool, and any supplementary tools work together without conflict

**Ongoing Technology Hygiene:**
- Close unnecessary applications and browser tabs during class to reduce system load and prevent notification interruptions
- Keep your teaching software updated, but do not update immediately before class (updates can introduce unexpected changes)
- Maintain a technology troubleshooting guide for students and share it proactively
- Record sessions whenever possible (with appropriate consent) as a safety net for students who experience technical difficulties

### 1.2 Learning Environment Design

**Virtual Classroom Setup:**
- Create a consistent visual environment: same background, same framing, same lighting for every session. Consistency helps students focus on content rather than noticing environmental changes.
- Use a professional but welcoming background. Physical bookshelves or a tidy workspace are preferable to virtual backgrounds, which can create visual artifacts and distraction.
- Position your camera at eye level. Looking up or down at the camera creates an unflattering angle and impedes the sense of eye contact.
- Frame yourself so that your head and shoulders fill approximately one-third to one-half of the screen. Too far away feels distant; too close feels invasive.

**Student Environment Considerations:**
- Acknowledge that students are learning in diverse environments: shared rooms, noisy households, limited desk space, inconsistent connectivity. Design your class to be tolerant of imperfect environments.
- Do not require cameras to be on for every session. Camera-on expectations can disadvantage students whose living situations are not conducive to being on camera.
- Provide asynchronous alternatives for participation that do not require real-time audio or video.

### 1.3 Setting Expectations

**First Week Essentials:**
- Publish a clear, detailed syllabus that specifically addresses remote learning expectations, technology requirements, and participation norms
- Hold a live orientation session dedicated solely to technology, navigation, and expectations (do not combine this with content instruction)
- Create a short video tour of your LMS course site showing where everything is
- Establish communication norms: How should students reach you? What is your response time commitment? When are your virtual office hours?
- Co-create community agreements with students about camera use, chat behavior, muting, and participation

## Part 2: Synchronous Instruction

### 2.1 Session Structure

**The 15-Minute Rule:**
Research consistently shows that attention spans in video-based instruction are shorter than in-person settings. Limit any single instructional mode to approximately 15 minutes before introducing a change of pace: a question, a poll, a discussion, a brief activity, or a media element.

**Effective Session Structure:**
1. **Opening (5 minutes)**: Warm welcome, agenda overview, connection to previous content, and a low-stakes engagement prompt (poll, quick question, chat response)
2. **Core Instruction Segment 1 (10-15 minutes)**: Direct instruction with visual support (slides, whiteboard, demonstration)
3. **Active Processing 1 (5-10 minutes)**: Breakout discussion, reflection prompt, problem-solving exercise, or think-pair-share
4. **Core Instruction Segment 2 (10-15 minutes)**: Second instructional block building on the first
5. **Active Processing 2 (5-10 minutes)**: Application activity, case study analysis, or collaborative task
6. **Closing (5 minutes)**: Key takeaways, preview of next session, assignment reminders, open questions

**What to Avoid:**
- Do not lecture for 50+ minutes continuously. Even excellent lecturers lose remote audiences after 15-20 minutes.
- Do not read from slides. If the content is on the slide, students can read it themselves. Use slides as visual anchors for your verbal explanation, not as scripts.
- Do not ignore the chat. Assign a chat monitor (TA, co-instructor, or rotating student role) if you cannot monitor it yourself while teaching.

### 2.2 Engagement Strategies

**Polling and Quick Checks:**
Use polls every 10-15 minutes to check understanding, gather opinions, or spark discussion. Polls serve triple duty: they provide formative assessment data, they give students a low-risk way to participate, and they break the passivity of watching a screen.

**Chat-Based Participation:**
The chat is a powerful engagement tool unique to remote instruction. Use it intentionally:
- Pose a question and ask everyone to type their answer but not send until you say "Go" (prevents anchoring bias)
- Ask for emoji reactions to check understanding or mood
- Have students post questions in the chat as they arise, then address them during natural break points
- Pair verbal discussion with chat discussion for students who are more comfortable writing than speaking

**Breakout Rooms:**
Breakout rooms are the remote equivalent of small group work. Use them effectively:
- Provide clear, written instructions before sending students to rooms (post instructions in the chat and in a shared document)
- Assign specific roles: facilitator, note-taker, reporter
- Set a timer and visit rooms briefly to check progress
- Keep breakout sessions to 5-10 minutes for discussions, 10-20 minutes for activities
- Always debrief after breakout rooms; otherwise students feel their work was pointless

**Cold Calling (Done Right):**
Random calling can increase engagement but must be done with care in remote settings:
- Announce at the start of the course that you will randomly call on students
- Give students the option to say "pass" without penalty
- Use a randomizer tool (see the Discussion Randomizer in this toolkit) rather than selecting students in a way that feels targeted
- Call on students for low-stakes questions, not high-stakes ones, to reduce anxiety

### 2.3 Screen Sharing Best Practices

- Use annotation tools to highlight, circle, and underline key content as you discuss it. Movement draws attention to where you want students to focus.
- Toggle between your face and your screen. Students need to see both your content and your expressions, but not necessarily simultaneously. Many platforms allow you to pin your video while sharing your screen.
- When demonstrating software or processes, slow down significantly. What feels natural speed to you is often too fast for students trying to follow along on their own screens.
- Use a clean desktop with no visible notification badges, personal bookmarks, or irrelevant applications.

## Part 3: Asynchronous Instruction

### 3.1 Video Content

**Recording Guidelines:**
- Keep videos short: 6-12 minutes is the optimal range. Break longer topics into multiple short videos.
- Use a conversational tone, not a lecture tone. Imagine you are explaining something to a student during office hours.
- Include your face via picture-in-picture or intro/outro. Students engage more when they can see their instructor.
- Add chapter markers or timestamps for longer videos so students can navigate to specific topics.
- Include captions. Auto-generated captions are a starting point but must be reviewed and corrected.

**Structuring Video Content:**
1. Open with a clear statement of what the video covers and why it matters
2. Present the content in logical chunks with clear transitions
3. Include pauses for reflection ("Take a moment to think about...")
4. End with a summary of key points and a connection to the next activity
5. Close with a specific prompt or question that students should bring to the next live session

### 3.2 Discussion Forums

**Designing Effective Discussion Prompts:**
- Ask questions that require critical thinking, not just recall. Replace "What is..." with "Why does..." or "What would happen if..."
- Require engagement with peers' posts, not just original posts. "Respond to at least two classmates" should specify what a quality response looks like.
- Provide a rubric or exemplar for discussion quality
- Participate in the discussion yourself. Faculty presence significantly increases the quality and frequency of student contributions.
- Vary discussion formats: standard posts, debates, role-plays, case analyses, peer review

**Managing Discussion Volume:**
- With large classes, create smaller discussion groups (8-12 students) to make the discussion feel more intimate and less overwhelming
- Summarize key themes from the discussion in your next synchronous session to demonstrate that asynchronous work matters
- Grade discussions on quality, not just completion. A thoughtful post is worth more than a lengthy but superficial one.

### 3.3 Assignment Design for Remote Learners

- Design assignments that can be completed with the technology students reliably have access to
- Provide flexible deadlines where possible: "Due by end of week" rather than "Due at 11:59 PM on Thursday" accommodates students in different time zones and with different schedules
- Use a variety of submission formats: written, audio recorded, video recorded, visual/infographic, presentation
- Build in low-stakes practice opportunities before high-stakes assessments
- Provide rubrics with every assignment so expectations are explicit
- Allow submission drafts and revision opportunities to support learning over performance pressure

## Part 4: Building Community and Connection

### 4.1 Creating Belonging

**Strategies:**
- Learn and use students' names. In remote settings, this requires deliberate effort. Use name tents (virtual backgrounds with names), seating charts in breakout rooms, and repeated practice.
- Share something about yourself as a person, not just as an instructor. Brief personal anecdotes build connection and model vulnerability.
- Create a "water cooler" space -- an informal discussion forum or chat channel where students can socialize about non-academic topics
- Celebrate milestones: birthdays, achievements, interesting projects
- Begin some sessions with informal conversation before jumping into content. The two minutes spent on "How is everyone doing?" yield disproportionate returns in community building.

### 4.2 Supporting Struggling Students

**Early Warning Signs in Remote Settings:**
- Decreasing participation in synchronous sessions (fewer chat contributions, camera off more often)
- Late or missing assignments
- Declining quality of work
- Absence from breakout rooms or collaborative activities
- No response to emails or messages

**Intervention Strategies:**
- Reach out early and privately. A simple "I noticed you missed the last session -- is everything okay?" can open a door.
- Offer flexibility before the student asks for it. Students who are struggling often do not ask for help until the situation is dire.
- Connect students with campus resources: counseling, tutoring, technology lending, food assistance
- Create multiple pathways to demonstrate learning so that one missed assignment does not create an insurmountable gap
- Check in with all students periodically, not just those who seem to be struggling. Some students mask their difficulties very well.

## Part 5: Assessment in Remote Environments

### 5.1 Rethinking Assessment

Remote environments make traditional proctored exams problematic. Rather than trying to replicate in-person testing conditions (which often leads to invasive proctoring software and student anxiety), consider alternative assessment approaches:

- **Open-book, open-note assessments**: Design questions that require application and analysis, not just recall
- **Project-based assessments**: Extended projects that demonstrate skill application over time
- **Portfolio assessments**: Curated collections of student work with reflective commentary
- **Oral examinations**: Brief video-call assessments where students explain their understanding
- **Collaborative assessments**: Group projects with individual accountability components
- **Process documentation**: Students document their problem-solving process, not just the answer

### 5.2 Academic Integrity

- Design assessments that are difficult to cheat on rather than investing in surveillance technology
- Use unique, personalized prompts rather than standardized questions
- Focus on process (drafts, reflections, explanations) rather than just final products
- Require students to explain their reasoning, either in writing or verbally
- Use plagiarism detection tools as educational tools (show students their similarity reports) rather than solely as enforcement tools
- Discuss academic integrity openly as a community value, not just a rule to be enforced

## Part 6: Equity and Access

### 6.1 Addressing the Digital Divide

- Survey students at the start of the course about their technology access: device type, internet reliability, shared devices, quiet study space
- Provide low-bandwidth alternatives for all content: downloadable materials, audio-only options, text-based alternatives to video
- Work with your institution to provide technology lending programs, Wi-Fi hotspots, and campus study spaces
- Design your course to work on mobile devices, not just laptops
- Avoid requiring specific software that costs money; use free, web-based alternatives

### 6.2 Time Zone Considerations

- Record all synchronous sessions and make them available within 24 hours
- Offer multiple synchronous session times if enrollment spans more than 3 time zones
- Set deadlines at the end of the day rather than during class time
- Be explicit about which time zone your deadlines reference
- Consider asynchronous-first design with optional synchronous sessions

### 6.3 Accessibility

- Caption all video content (auto-captions are a starting point but must be reviewed)
- Provide alt text for all images in course materials
- Use heading structures in documents and LMS pages for screen reader navigation
- Ensure color is not the only means of conveying information
- Test your course site with keyboard-only navigation
- Provide materials in advance so students using assistive technology can prepare
- Ask students about accessibility needs at the start of the course, privately and non-judgmentally
